Issue overview

Bias can change the story.

The problem can begin before a courtroom. It can shape what happens at school, how young people are described, and what choices they are given.

What is the school-to-prison pipeline?

The ACLU calls it a pattern in which students are pushed out of school and into the juvenile or criminal legal system. Harsh discipline rules and police responses to behavior that a school could handle can play a part. The ACLU says students of color are especially vulnerable to unfair discipline.

This matters because a student who needs help can end up with missed classes and a record instead. It raises a question from our research sheet: what would happen if schools offered more support before punishment?

5.6×
U.S. youth placement, 2023

Black youth were 5.6 times as likely as white youth to be held in juvenile facilities, according to The Sentencing Project's report. This is a national rate comparison. It does not explain every individual case.

Where can bias enter a case?

Racial bias happens when assumptions about race affect how someone is seen or treated. The Innocence Project explains that bias contributes to wrongful convictions, including through unreliable cross-racial eyewitness identifications. The Equal Justice Initiative connects today's criminal legal system to a longer history of racial inequality.

Words matter, too

Our planning sheet asks how news stories can change the way people see teenagers of color who are accused of a crime. A headline is only one version of a person's story. We want readers to ask whose point of view is missing.

What could support look like?

The Sentencing Project describes community-based alternatives for youth that can produce better outcomes than incarceration in many cases. Support, education, and a chance to grow belong in the conversation.

Art can also help people keep a sense of identity. Rehabilitation Through the Arts runs arts programs in prisons and describes how participants develop skills and express themselves. Its programs are an example of what art can offer; its results should not be treated as proof about every incarcerated teenager.

What is happening in Georgia?

The Georgia Innocence Project works to correct and prevent wrongful convictions. Its work includes legal representation, support after release, community education, and policy reform. It gives this project a real local connection.

What can we do?

Start with the sources below. Compare what they say, notice which numbers describe youth and which describe adults, and talk about solutions that give young people a fair chance. Sharing an accurate story is a small but real way to challenge an unfair label.
